## 1. Why this upgrade is the one reviewers will ask for

The current study discriminates **prevalent, self-reported stroke** cross-sectionally. A high
AUROC there mostly measures how well a case-mix separates people who already had a stroke from
people who did not; it is not a claim about predicting a first event, and it cannot support the
deployment language (screening, triage, referral) that NPJ Digital Medicine expects. The
`docs/SELF_AUDIT.md` item O1 already flags this as the caveat "most likely to overturn" the
headline. Everything below quantifies whether that caveat can be removed rather than reworded.

## 2. Ceiling on incident events, measured on the tables already harmonised

| cohort | country | persons | person-waves | at risk at first determinable wave (stroke=0) | prevalent at entry | **incident cases** | cumulative incidence | median follow-up (waves) | incidence /1,000 wave-intervals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CHARLS | CN | 24,458 | 71,694 | 23,776 | 682 | **1,160** | 4.88% | 3 | 24.1 |
| KLoSA | KR | 11,115 | 65,494 | 10,792 | 323 | **536** | 4.97% | 8 | 9.5 |
| MHAS | MX | 27,092 | 91,709 | 26,527 | 565 | **933** | 3.52% | 3 | 14.4 |
| NHANES | US | 18,191 | 18,191 | 17,407 | 784 | **0** | — | — | — |

Pooled incident events from the three panels: **2,629**. Against the frozen 14-feature profile
that is **188 events per variable** pooled — far above the classical 10-20 EPV floor, so sample
size is not the obstacle. Per held-out cohort under leave-one-country-out the fit sees the other
two panels (1,469 / 2,093 / 1,696 events; EPV 105 / 150 / 121), and the *test* set contains 536–
1,163 events, which is enough for a stable AUROC (a 1,000-event test set gives an AUROC standard
error of roughly 0.015–0.02 at this discrimination level).

**Verdict on power: the incident design is comfortably feasible on the data already in hand.**

## 3. What NHANES can and cannot contribute

NHANES yields exactly **zero** incident events, because its harmonised table is one row per
participant per cycle with no linked repeat interview (`persons_2plus_waves = 0`). Options, in
ascending honesty:

- *Not admissible:* treating NHANES cycles 2015-16 / 2017-18 / 2021-23 as a panel. They are
  independent repeated cross-sections — different people. Any "incident" model built that way
  is a mislabeled prevalence model.
- *Admissible:* NHANES-linked **adult participant cohort (P-File) mortality/prospective files**
  do exist as a separate download and do follow the same SEQN; that is the only route by which
  the US arm enters a prospective design. It is a separate data-acquisition step, not a script change.
- *Admissible and cheap:* keep NHANES where it is — as the cross-sectional derivation and the
  within-country temporal-transport arm — and state so.

Consequence: a four-country *incident* LOCO is not available. The honest incident arm is
**three panels (CN / KR / MX)**, with NHANES retained as the cross-sectional US arm. That is a
design change in the manuscript's structure, not merely a new table.

## 4. Blockers that are real, ranked by cost

**(a) Mortality / competing risk — resolved 2026-11-03 by rebuilding the censoring rule.**
"Stroke ever reported" is a lifetime item, so between two waves an event can be interrupted by
death, and an incident model needs death to censor correctly. This section has been wrong twice,
and both corrections are kept visible:

*First error (2026-11-02):* the report claimed CHARLS carried no death variable. Its regex looked
for `death|died|exit|alive`; the harmonised gate names the field `radyear`.

*Second error (2026-11-03):* the report then concluded China was "ready" because `radyear` exists.
It exists, but it is **not usable as a mortality source**: it is populated for 402 of 25,586 CHARLS
respondents (1.6% of wave records after merging) and its values stop at 2014, two waves short of
the panel's end. MHAS is the opposite — 27% of wave records carry a death date. A design censored
only on death *dates* would therefore have treated most Chinese deaths as ordinary attrition.

The field that actually carries mortality is the interview-status item, identically coded in both
panels: `4` = non-response but alive, `5` = died this wave, `6` = died previous wave, `9` = vital
status unknown.

| cohort | records with iwstat 5/6 | of which carry a fieldwork date | death-date coverage | usable censoring signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CHARLS | 3,581 | **0 (0.0%)** | 1.6% (`radyear`) | status only → window-bounded |
| MHAS | 22,036 | **0 (0.0%)** | 27.0% (`radyear`) | status + date for a minority |
| KLoSA | field absent | — | — | nothing on disk |

Because a death-coded record has no date, the death can only be *bounded*: it happened between the
last date the person was known alive and that wave's fieldwork window. The window is taken from the
data itself — the median interview date of the dated records in each wave (CHARLS 2011.54 / 2013.63
/ 2015.54 / 2018.54; MHAS 2001.46 / 2003.54 / 2012.88 / 2015.88 / 2018.88 / 2021.96) — and the
interval is censored at the **midpoint** of the death window. Crediting the full gap would
over-count exposure; cutting at the last interview would under-count it. Effect of using status
rather than dates alone: Chinese death-terminated intervals go from 338 to 1,557, Mexican from
6,529 to 6,913.

Korea remains blocked, and the block is now a disclosed design asymmetry rather than a to-do: none
of the nine KLoSA main wave workbooks contains a vital-status or death field (checked column by
column; `w0{n}panel_n` and `w0{n}mniw_y/_m/_d` are all the waves carry), and mortality lives in the
separate `w0{2..8}_exit_e` exit workbooks, which are not on disk. Korea contributes 54,379
intervals with **zero** death-terminated rows and `censor_mode="last_interview"` in every one of
them. That is not neutral: censoring decedents at their last interview under-credits their
exposure, which biases the Korean incidence *upwards* relative to a death-censored computation, and
it is why Korea must be reported as the panel with a different censoring rule, not pooled as if
the three panels shared one.

A finding that helps instead of hurts: Korea's stroke item is a **first-report** item, not a
repeated lifetime item (traced in the raw waves - of the 859 ever-positive persons, 322 answer yes
in wave 1, only 79 again in wave 2, and 258 are skipped to missing). For an incident design that is
the right structure, since each person can contribute at most one event; and it shows the
manuscript's line that Korea's item is merely "a stricter ever-diagnosis question" was wrong.

**(b) Unequal wave intervals make "per wave" the wrong time scale.** Verified interview years:
CHARLS 2011 / 2013 / 2015 / 2018 (intervals 2, 2, 3 y); MHAS 2001 / 2003 / 2012 / 2015 / 2018 /
2021-22 (intervals 2, **9**, 3, 3, 3 y — the 2007 release is not in this harmonised file); KLoSA
is biennial 2006→2022 (documented; wave files not re-parsed here). The incidence column in §2 is
therefore *per wave-interval* and cohort rates are not directly comparable; a real model must use
elapsed years as the time scale.

**(c) Left truncation from the age-restricted chronic-disease block.** MHAS asks the condition
block only from age 50 and CHARLS from ~45, so "first determinable wave = 0" is only meaningful
for people already inside the asked window at entry; and an incident case found at wave 3 of a
person who entered at wave 3 is a **prevalent-but-newly-detected** case, not incident. The
entry-wave distribution shows how much this matters: CHARLS admits 3,839 persons whose first
determinable wave is 4, and MHAS admits 5,905 at wave 3 and 4,812 at wave 5. The correct rule is
to enter follow-up only at the first wave where the person was **both interviewed and inside the
asked window**, which shrinks the denominators above — 2,629 is the ceiling, not the expectation.

**(d) Interval censoring and no subtype.** A lifetime "ever told you had a stroke" item observed
every 2–3 years gives interval-censored event times, not exact times, and gives no ischaemic vs
haemorrhagic split. Both must stay in the Limitations even after the upgrade.

**(e) The outcome is still self-reported.** The shared-reporting-bias caveat (O2) is unaffected by
making the design prospective.

## 5. Two corrections this diagnostic turned up, independent of the upgrade

1. **The manuscript's no-weights statement is wrong.** `manuscript.md` Methods and
   `INTL_DATA_RUNBOOK.md` both say the international arm carries no design weights "because the
   harmonised files do not carry design weights". Verified false: CHARLS ships `r{n}wtresp`/
   `r{n}wtrespa/b`, MHAS ships `r{n}wtresp`, and KLoSA ships `w0Xwgt_*`. Design-weighted
   prevalence, and weighted LOCO evaluation, are available for all four cohorts — which matters
   because the unweighted prevalences (US 4.3% / CN 4.4% / KR 1.3% / MX 3.7%) are currently
   presented as if they were population estimates.
2. **KLoSA's "missing" features were partly a coding bug, now documented separately.**
   `std_KLoSA.csv` had `income_pir`, `bmi` and `high_cholesterol` at 0% non-null, so every Korean
   row got all three imputed from the training countries. See
   `results/reports/intl_arm_data_defects_2026-11-02.md`: `w{v}C105`/`w{v}C107` give an observed
   BMI on 98% of rows, and the education/alcohol/marital columns were actively wrong. The
   incident tally above is unaffected (Korea's *outcome* mapping was already correct: same
   859 events, 11,115 persons), but every LOCO discrimination number for Korea has to be
   re-estimated on the corrected features.

### 8.1 The dataset

Person identity is `cohort|pid` throughout (a bare `pid` collides across panels). Pooled:
**172,727 intervals from 53,638 adults, 2,629 first-reported strokes, 500,965 observed
person-years.**

| panel | country | at-risk persons | prevalent at entry (excluded) | intervals | **incident events** | death-terminated intervals | person-years | median gap (y) | p95 gap (y) | incidence /1,000 PY | design-weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CHARLS | CN | 23,776 | 682 | 47,387 | **1,160** | 1,557 | 113,136 | 2.08 | 3.08 | 10.25 | 10.19 |
| KLoSA | KR | 10,792 | 323 | 54,379 | **536** | **0** (no mortality on disk) | 112,883 | 2.00 | 2.17 | 4.75 | 4.14 |
| MHAS | MX | 26,525 | 565 | 70,961 | **933** | 6,913 | 274,946 | 3.00 | 9.33 | 3.39 | 3.36 |

The event counts reproduce the §2 ceiling exactly (1,160 / 536 / 933, pooled 2,629), which is the
check that rebuilding on dates and vital status did not silently lose or invent events. What
changed is the denominator: the tally counted wave-intervals, this counts elapsed person-years,
and the three panels turn out to differ **threefold in incidence** (China 10.25, Korea 4.75,
Mexico 3.39 per 1,000 PY) — a fact the per-wave tally could not see.

Baseline (first-interval) missingness: every one of the ten shared concepts is observed on ≥97.8%
of persons (worst: education 2.2%, marital 1.5%); `income_pir` 26.5%, `bmi` 42.1%,
`high_cholesterol` 100% missing — which is why the primary incident feature set is the ten
concepts and the wider set is a labelled sensitivity.

### 8.2 Horizon accounting, stated as exclusions rather than as negatives

For a 4-year risk model, of 53,638 adults: **1,723** had a first stroke inside 4 years (label 1),
**39,624** were followed at least 4 years stroke-free (label 0), **5,196 died inside 4 years
without a reported stroke** and **7,095 vanished before 4 years**; those two groups, 12,291
adults (22.9%), are **excluded**, not coded as non-events. Counting a competing death as "no
stroke" would be the single largest fabricable number in this design, so it is the first thing a
reviewer should be able to find.

### 8.3 Two implementation errors caught in this arm before any number was quoted

1. *Interval length is a covariate, not a nuisance.* The first version omitted
   `exposure_years`, so the model was asked for the probability of an event in an interval without
   being told its length, in panels that mix 2-year and 20-year gaps. Adding it moved China's
   held-out interval AUROC from 0.695 to 0.719.
2. *Composing a 4-year risk from 1-year steps is extrapolation.* With `exposure_years` in the
   model, a 1-year step sits at the edge of that covariate's range: the boosted trees returned
   hazards of 0.001–0.005 for nearly everyone and their horizon AUROC fell **below chance**
   (LightGBM 0.439 on China). The horizon is now composed over the interval length the training
   panels actually use (their median gap), advancing age between steps, and `realised_horizon_years`
   is written next to every row so the composed horizon cannot be mistaken for exactly 4.0 years.
   A single-interval variant (`level="horizon_4y_single_interval"`) is reported beside it, so the
   reader can see how much the answer depends on the composition rule.

### 8.4 Results of the three-panel incident LOCO

Source: `data/processed/intl/incident/incident_loco_common_linear_H4.csv` (person-clustered
bootstrap, B=2000, seed 20260701+7; 10 shared concepts; `exposure_years` and
`t_since_entry_years` as covariates; no design weights in this table).

**Interval level** — one row per person-interval, the model's own likelihood unit:

| held-out panel | intervals | events | interval prevalence | logistic AUROC [95% CI] | best model (AUROC) | mean predicted | scaled Brier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CN | 47,387 | 1,160 | 2.45% | **0.7188 [0.7049, 0.7326]** | XGBoost 0.7354 | 0.0100 | −0.0001 |
| KR | 54,379 | 536 | 0.99% | **0.6447 [0.6228, 0.6674]** | **age+sex only 0.6550** | 0.0110 | +0.0019 |
| MX | 70,961 | 933 | 1.31% | **0.6602 [0.6434, 0.6767]** | logistic 0.6602 | 0.0685 | −0.6883 |

Three things in this table are not incidental:

1. **Korea is predicted best by age and sex alone.** Every multivariable model is beaten by the
   two-variable baseline (0.6550 vs 0.6447 for the ten-concept logistic). In China the comorbidity
   block is worth +0.096 AUROC over the same baseline (0.7188 vs 0.6225) and in Mexico +0.077
   (0.6602 vs 0.5835). So the value of the cardiometabolic profile is not a property of stroke, it
   is a property of which panel you are in.
2. **Mexico's interval-level failure is an extrapolation artefact, and it is worth naming.** Its
   mean predicted interval hazard is 0.0685 against a 0.0131 observed rate because Mexican gaps are
   long (median 3.0 y, 95th percentile 9.3 y, maximum 20.6 y) while the training panels it was
   fitted on - China and Korea - have maximum gaps of 7.2 y and 16.3 y with 95th percentiles of
   3.1 y and 2.2 y. A model given `exposure_years` as a covariate is asked to extrapolate it. The
   horizon-scale table below, which never asks for an interval length outside the training range,
   puts Mexico's mean predicted 4-year risk at 0.0296 against 0.0310 observed - the single largest
   calibration error in the interval table is an artefact of the interval scale, not of the model.
3. **Scaled Brier is at or near zero everywhere.** "Brier 0.013 looks good" was the prevalent arm's
   trap and the incident arm does not escape it: against the held-out panel's own base rate the
   model explains 0.0-0.2% of the variance in China and Korea.

**Horizon level** — one row per person, 4-year risk composed over the training panel's own median
interval length (`realised_horizon_years` is reported because Korea's composition lands on 3.0 y,
not 4.0 y):

| held-out panel | evaluable persons | observed 4-y risk | AUROC (logistic) | best model (AUROC) | mean predicted | cal. intercept | NB at 5% | NB treat-all at 5% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CN | 16,415 | 6.09% | 0.6787 [0.6618, 0.6950] | elastic net / logistic 0.6787 | 0.0176 | +1.71 | +0.00218 | **+0.01143** |
| KR | 8,909 | 2.55% (3-y realised) | 0.6833 | XGBoost 0.6981 | 0.0147 | +0.76 | +0.00047 | −0.02581 |
| MX | 16,023 | 3.10% | 0.6572 | logistic 0.6572 | 0.0296 | −0.63 | +0.00294 | −0.01998 |

Read against the two defaults a clinician actually has (treat-all, treat-none):

* In **China** the frozen model's net benefit at a 5% threshold is **+0.00218, while simply treating
  everyone scores +0.01143**. Because the frozen model predicts a 1.8% mean 4-year risk in a
  population whose true risk is 6.1%, almost nobody crosses the threshold, and the model is worse
  than the crudest policy. This is the single most important result in the incident arm and it is
  exactly what the prevalent arm's AUROC concealed.
* In **Korea** net benefit is +0.0005, i.e. indistinguishable from treating nobody.
* In **Mexico** the model does beat both defaults (+0.00294 vs −0.01998 and 0), because Mexico's
  baseline risk happens to sit close to what a China+Korea-trained model predicts.

**The class-weighted forest fails in the incident arm too**: mean predicted 4-year risk 0.39-0.52
against observed 0.026-0.061, scaled Brier −3.9 to −7.1, net benefit equal to treat-all. Its AUROC
is only ~0.02 below the unweighted forest, so the damage is entirely in the probability, never in
the ranking. This is the ablation the reviewer asked for, reproduced on a different outcome.

### 8.5 Frozen, offset, recalibrated, or retrained — the experiment this design makes possible

Because the failure is in the level and not in the ranking, the incident arm can answer a question
the prevalent arm could only assert: how much local data does each repair cost, and which repair is
worth it? `incident_adaptation.py` holds out one panel entirely, fits the model on the other two,
then gives each strategy a random sample of the held-out panel's **persons** (never intervals) and
scores on the rest. Results are in `data/processed/intl/incident/incident_adaptation.csv`
(3 model families × 3 panels × 5 local fractions × 5 replicates = 909 rows).

**Net benefit at a 5% four-year-risk threshold, logistic model, 5% local sample (946 Chinese /
479 Korean / 1,258 Mexican adults):**

| panel | frozen | intercept-only ("offset") | intercept+slope ("recalibrate") | full local retrain | treat everyone | treat no one |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CN | +0.0022 | **+0.0168** | +0.0167 | +0.0058 | +0.0114 | 0 |
| KR | +0.0005 | +0.0006 | +0.0006 | −0.0006 | −0.0258 | 0 |
| MX | **+0.0030** | +0.0000 | −0.0000 | +0.0007 | −0.0198 | 0 |

Four findings, each of which is checkable in that table:

1. **One number repairs China.** The frozen model predicts a mean 4-year risk of 0.0176 where the
   truth is 0.0610, so at a 5% threshold it flags 2.1% of people and scores below treat-everyone.
   Moving only the log-odds intercept - fitted on a 5% person sample, but equivalently from a
   published local incidence rate, since the estimator needs only the target's base rate - raises
   the mean prediction to 0.0409, the flagged share to 24.7%, and net benefit to +0.0168, which is
   45% above treat-everyone. The AUROC does not move: at the interval scale the largest |Δ AUROC|
   against the frozen model on the same rows is **exactly 0.000000** across all 225 replications of
   each of the three model families, and at the composed horizon it is ≤ 7.3e-4 — the residual is not
   a change in discrimination but the fact that stepping a hazard across intervals is not itself a
   monotone map of the score. This is the
   definition of a monotone map, and it is the reason a "recalibrated model" can never be sold as an
   accuracy improvement.
2. **Full local retraining is actively harmful at every sample size tried.** Paired against the
   frozen model on identical evaluation rows (45 model × panel × replicate combinations per point),
   retraining on 2% of the panel (378 Chinese / 191 Korean / 503 Mexican adults) loses a **mean
   0.0837 AUROC (median 0.0757, range
   0.0015-0.2391, worse in 45/45 cases)**; at 5% it loses 0.0535 (43/45 worse), at 10% 0.0199
   (36/45), at 25% 0.0152 (32/45), and even at 50% (9,455 Chinese adults) it is still a median 0.0009
   behind with 24/45 combinations worse. Its net benefit in China
   tops out at +0.0076 (mean over replications, best local size) against the
   single intercept's +0.0168. The practical rule is the opposite of the intuition: small local data
   should buy a calibration parameter, not a model.
3. **Mexico is the control case, and it is the most informative column.** Mexico's frozen model is
   already right in the large (mean predicted 0.0296 vs observed 0.0308 four-year risk), so every
   adaptation makes it worse - offset drops net benefit from +0.0030 to +0.0000 because correcting
   the *interval-level* hazard, which Mexico's long gaps inflate, over-corrects the *person-level*
   risk that was fine. A country whose base rate is close to the training pools should ship the
   model unchanged. The remedy must be applied on the scale the decision is made at; this is the
   one place the interval and horizon scales genuinely disagree.
4. **Korea has no repair available, because its problem is information, not calibration.** Every
   strategy sits within +0.0006/-0.0006 of zero net benefit at 5%, and the ceiling is visible in
   §8.4: age and sex alone predict a first Korean stroke as well as the ten-concept model. No amount
   of local recalibration manufactures discrimination the features do not carry.
5. **The offset's benefit is flat in sample size, which is the operationally important part.** In
   China it is +0.01666 at 2% local persons and +0.01662 at 50% - indistinguishable. One
   aggregate incidence figure is worth as much as 9,000 labelled person-interviews, because the only
   thing being corrected is the intercept and that parameter converges immediately.

### 8.6 What this changes about the manuscript

The prevalent arm's headline - held-out AUROC 0.735-0.799, "the simple model transports best" - is
not wrong, but it is no longer the study. Three consequences:

1. **The outcome, the unit of analysis and the time scale all change**, and with them the numbers:
   247,088 respondent-waves become 172,727 person-intervals from 53,638 adults, and a cross-sectional
   prevalence becomes an incidence rate that differs threefold between the panels.
2. **The claim that survives is narrower and more useful.** Discrimination transports (AUROC
   0.645-0.719 per interval, 0.657-0.683 for four-year risk, held-out); absolute risk does not; and
   the gap between those two facts is exactly one intercept, which a health system can estimate from
   its own registry rather than from patient-level data. That is a transferable finding. The claim
   that "added complexity buys discrimination and costs calibration" does not survive either, in a
   sharper form than expected: on held-out data every ensemble is at or below the penalised logistic
   (mean 0.630-0.658 vs 0.675), and the boosted trees are better only in China (0.735 vs 0.719). But
   internally the boosted trees *are* better - 0.737 vs 0.680 under a person-clustered CV - so the
   correct statement is not "complexity buys nothing", it is "complexity buys discrimination at home
   and loses it abroad" (§8.7, item 1).
3. **Deployment language goes.** At the thresholds a screening programme would use, the frozen
   incident model's net benefit is below treat-everyone in China and indistinguishable from zero in
   Korea and Mexico. No version of this analysis supports a sentence about a clinician-facing
   tool, and the manuscript must say what it does support instead: that a transported model should
   be checked in the large before it is trusted, and that the cheapest check is also the most
   effective repair.

   AUROC 0.7375 (XGBoost), 0.7371 (LightGBM), 0.7366 (gradient boosting), 0.7276 (random forest)
   against 0.6804 (logistic), i.e. the boosted learners *are* 5-6 points better at home - and their
   mean held-out AUROC is 0.658 / 0.630 / 0.652 / 0.665 against the logistic model's 0.675. Decay
   (internal minus held-out): +0.005 elastic net, +0.006 logistic, +0.008 spline, +0.063 random
   forest, +0.080 XGBoost, +0.085 gradient boosting, +0.107 LightGBM. A row-level CV gives the same
   internal values to within 0.002, so the gradient is not a leakage artefact. The age+sex baseline
   shows negative decay (−0.031), a composition effect: pooling three panels with threefold different
   incidence flattens a demographic gradient that is cleaner inside any one country.
2. **Four sensitivity arms, all published.** No time term; extended 13-concept feature set; six-year
   horizon; design-weighted fit and evaluation. Logistic horizon AUROC is stable to ≤0.006 except
   Korea under weights (interval 0.612 vs 0.645). The weighted Mexican arm is the instructive one:
   its interval-scale mean predicted hazard rises to 0.197 against 0.013 observed (Brier skill
   −6.67), because weighting up the long 2003-2012 gaps amplifies the same extrapolation the
   unweighted model already failed at. At six years China's observed risk rises to 0.087 while the
   frozen prediction reaches only 0.027, so the model stays below treat-all (+0.0142 vs +0.0390) and
   the intercept correction remains the binding intervention.
3. **A sixth defect in our own code, found while re-quoting the ablation table.**
   `rf_probability_ablation.py` computed its `--features` concept list and then passed the whole
   encoded frame to the model, so the "common" and "extended" runs were byte-identical and the
   NHANES-only race columns leaked into the run that claimed to be the harmonised subset. After the
   fix, the qualitative conclusion holds (the class weight, not capacity, is what destroyed the
   forest's probabilities: Mexico Brier 0.306 weighted against 0.036 unweighted, for 0.002 AUROC) but
   one number changes materially: on the properly restricted ten-concept set, **Mexico's logistic
   Brier skill is negative (−0.008)**, not the +0.004 previously printed. The harmonised subset
   carries no usable absolute-risk information for Mexico at all; restoring income/BMI/cholesterol is
   what turns it positive. Any table quoted from the pre-fix run is superseded by
   `results/tables/rf_probability_ablation_common_2026-11-03.csv`.
4. **What remains genuinely open.** Korea's exit-interview workbooks (death dates) are still not on

   events are a lower bound. The authors must supply and verify subject-specific references, confirm
